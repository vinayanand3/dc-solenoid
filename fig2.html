7 · Automotive application: a solenoid-driven locking pin

The same solenoid, built push-type: a rod through its pole drives either a hardened pin into holes in a lock ring, or, through a shift fork, a dog collar whose teeth mesh with teeth on the ring, which is closer to how an electronic locker works. The collar slides on a splined hub fixed to the housing, so engaging it locks the ring. Energize to engage, spring to release. All dimensions and loads are illustrative.

Who carries the torque

The solenoid is an actuator, not a structural part. Once the pin is in, the ring's torque T reaches the housing through the pin in shear, F = T / r (here r = 35 mm, so 250 N·m is about 7 kN on the pin), and through its bore. The solenoid's few newtons only move and hold the pin. With the dog clutch the same torque is shared by the flanks of twelve teeth (the readout shows the total tangential load, T / r), which is why production lockers use dog teeth or a locking collar sized for the axle's torque rather than a single pin.

What decides whether it engages

Alignment: a hole (or a tooth gap) must come within the capture window, the clearance plus the chamfer: 2.2 mm for the pin, 2.8 mm for the 12-tooth dog clutch, whose teeth fill 40 % of the pitch. Force: magnetic force at the open gap must beat preload and friction. Friction and contamination raise the force needed. Tolerance stack-up in the bracket, bore, pin and ring positions eats into the clearance and the stroke: too little and the pin can't find the hole, too much and it engages shallow. Speed difference: too fast and the pin skates over the holes or catches only a corner.

What this model leaves out

The ring is driven at a set speed with a set torque, not by a vehicle model. Impacts between pin and hole edge, wear, chamfer cam forces and backlash are not simulated; the chamfer is a capture window. Pin friction is μ times the shear force. These are illustrative numbers, not a real locker's specification.
