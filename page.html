    The equations behind it

    Tagged by how far to trust them: exact principle idealized approximation illustrative

    Electrical: Faraday's law for the coil exact principle

    V = R·i + dλ/dt

    λ is the flux linkage, N·Φ. It depends on both the current and the plunger position, λ(i, x), so a moving plunger adds a speed term: dλ/dt = (∂λ/∂i)·di/dt + (∂λ/∂x)·dx/dt. That second term is why the current dips while the plunger travels, a dip drivers use to confirm motion.

    With the plunger held still and the iron linear, λ = L·i and this becomes V = R·i + L·di/dt, so after switch-on i(t) = (V/R)(1 − e−t/τ), τ = L/R. The current cannot jump to V/R because the field's energy, ½·L·i², has to be supplied first.

    The simulation integrates λ directly (implicit Euler, 5 µs steps) and gets i from the magnetic circuit, so inductance changing with gap and saturation is handled without a separate L.

    Magnetic circuit: Ampère's law, reluctance idealized approximation

    N·i ≈ Φ·(ℛgap + ℛiron),  ℛ = ℓ/(μ·A)

    The coil's magnetomotive force N·i drives flux Φ round the closed loop: pole → working gap → plunger → front plate → can → back plate → pole. Iron has μr in the thousands, so 90 mm of iron here adds the same reluctance as about 0.04 mm of air. The gap dominates, which is why closing it raises the flux so sharply.

    B ≈ μ0·N·i / geff,  geff = g + 0.30 mm + ℓiron/μr

    geff is an effective total gap for this simplified loop, not the visible gap alone: it lumps the sleeve's radial gap and joints (0.30 mm, illustrative) and the iron path. Assumptions: no saturation (unless you enable it), no leakage or fringing, the same effective area A all round the loop.

    Saturation (advanced). Real soft iron flattens out near 1.5 to 2 T. With saturation on, the iron follows B = Bs·tanh(μ0μrH / Bs) with Bs = 1.6 T, so neither flux nor force can keep growing as the gap closes or the current rises. With it off, the page warns whenever the ideal B passes about 1.5 T.

    Force on the plunger idealized approximation

    Fmag = B²·A / (2μ0)  ⇒  Fmag ≈ μ0·N²·i²·A / (2·geff²)

    The first form (Maxwell stress on a uniform gap) also equals −∂W/∂x at constant flux linkage, so it holds with saturation; the second is the constant-current form for linear iron and one dominant uniform gap. It is an educational approximation, not a sizing equation: real force-stroke curves depend on pole shape (conical poles flatten the curve), leakage, fringing, saturation, coil heating and the drive circuit. The force always pulls toward a smaller gap, whichever way the current flows, because it goes as B².

    Fspring = Fpreload + k·x    Fnet = Fmag − Fspring − Fload − Ffriction    m·ẍ = Fnet − c·ẋ

    x is plunger travel, positive toward the pole (pull-in). The ribbon load is a constant-force spring, so Fload doesn't change with stroke; its drum's inertia is ignored. Friction is Coulomb (0.4 N static, 0.3 N sliding, illustrative) and c = 2 N·s/m. Both stops are hard, with a restitution of 0.2, so the plunger bounces slightly but never passes through a part.

    Switching off: flyback and release exact principle

    When the MOSFET opens, the coil keeps its current flowing and its voltage reverses until something conducts. A plain diode clamps the coil at about −0.8 V, so the current decays slowly (τ ≈ L/R) and the flux holds the plunger for many milliseconds. A diode in series with a 33 V clamp lets the coil swing to about −34 V: the current, the flux and the force collapse several times faster and the plunger releases sooner, at the price of a drain voltage of V + 34 V the MOSFET must be rated for. With no flyback path the drain avalanches at its breakdown voltage; release is fast but every turn-off dumps the field's energy into the MOSFET.

    Electrical decay and mechanical return are separate events: the spring only starts the plunger back once the falling magnetic force drops below the spring force plus load.

    Drive: PWM and peak-and-hold illustrative

    Duty cycle here is the PWM duty of the MOSFET, modelled as an average voltage d·V (with diode freewheeling in the off time). The PWM period is assumed much shorter than L/R. With peak-and-hold on, the coil gets full voltage to pull in, then the duty to hold: a seated plunger has a tiny gap and needs little current, so hold power and heating drop sharply.

    What is simulated and what is drawn

    • Simulated (one model, every number): coil current with inductance that changes with gap; flux linkage, flux density and force from the lumped magnetic circuit; optional iron saturation; the three flyback paths and drain voltage; PWM averaging and peak-and-hold; spring, constant load, Coulomb friction, damping, hard stops; the energy ledger; the fault cases; the lock-pin contact in Fig 2.
    • Idealized: one uniform working gap with no leakage or fringing; a fixed parasitic gap; one effective area round the loop; no eddy currents in the iron; no hysteresis or remanence (the flux returns to zero when the current does); coil temperature is a setting, not a thermal simulation.
    • Illustrative only: flux lines are schematic paths whose opacity follows the computed B, not FEA field plots; the fringing arcs at the gap; the current-flow dots on the wires; the wire ends in the cut winding (drawn larger than a real 600-turn coil's wire); force arrows are scaled for legibility; the drawing keeps the Ø16 mm geometry when you pick a smaller pole area.
    • No values here are measured data or a real part's specification, and nothing implies compliance with any standard.
